Layered drawing

Say itsoo-gee-YAH-mah/sɯɡijama/

How do you draw a flowchart so that every arrow points down and few of them cross?

Example: the steps of a software build, each arrow from a step to one that waits for it. A force-directed drawing would scatter them; a layered drawing puts each step on a row, below everything it waits for.

The ideaOne hard problem, split into easier ones

  
Drawing a directed graph well is hard all at once, so Sugiyama, Tagawa and Toda split it into phases, each solved on its own. Break the loops by turning a few arrows around. Give every vertex a layer so arrows point down, keeping them short. Cut long arrows into pieces at every layer they cross. Order each layer to avoid crossings. Then choose exact positions along each layer. Grafluent’s sugiyamaLayout() uses a minimum feedback arc heuristic for the first phase, Gansner’s network simplex for the layers, barycentre sweeps for the order and Brandes and Köpf’s method for the positions.

  
Trees need much less. A tree already has layers, its depths, and nothing to cross. Reingold and Tilford draw each subtree on its own, then push neighbouring subtrees together until their outlines touch at the minimum spacing, and centre each parent over its children. reingoldTilfordLayout() does this in linear time, and draws two subtrees of the same shape identically, which is what makes a tidy tree look tidy.


  
Where it breaksCrossings, and wide layers

  
Finding the order with the fewest crossings is NP-hard even for two layers, so the sweeps are a heuristic: they usually do well, and the drawing reports its crossingCount so you can tell. A graph with many arrows between distant layers fills the drawing with bend points, and wide layers make a short, broad picture. For graphs that are mostly a tree, the tree layout gives a cleaner result; for graphs with no clear direction, a force-directed layout suits better.

  
Loops have no right answer: which arrows to turn around is a choice, and the smallest such set is itself NP-hard to find. The drawing marks them in reversedEdges, so the reader isn’t misled into thinking every arrow points down.
