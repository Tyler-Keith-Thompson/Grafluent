Community hierarchy

A company has departments, and departments have teams. Can an algorithm find both layers?

What it’s forGroups at more than one size

Community detection splits a network into groups. But real groups nest: teams inside departments, neighbourhoods inside cities, topics inside fields. A community hierarchy keeps every layer and remembers which group each smaller group came from.

Reach for it when you want summaries at several scales, as retrieval over a knowledge graph does: a short summary per department and a detailed one per team. Or when one community is simply too big to be useful and you want it broken down.

Compared withOther ways to get layered groups

Hierarchical Leiden gives you control through the cap. Louvain’s levels come free but you don’t choose them. Girvan–Newman gives the richest tree on networks small enough to afford it.

Example: twenty-four people in six teams of four, two teams to a department, with a liaison between each pair of departments.

Step throughOne level at a time

The ideaCluster, then cluster again inside anything too big

Run Leiden on the whole network. Every community that has more people than the cap is taken on its own, as a network by itself with only the pairs inside it, and Leiden runs again on that. Its communities become the children of the big one. Repeat until every community fits under the cap, or Leiden keeps a community in one piece, in which case it is final however big.

Running on a piece by itself matters. Inside a department, the pairs to other departments are gone, so modularity sees the two teams clearly and splits them. On the whole network those same teams were worth keeping together.

The result numbers its communities level by level. communities(atLevel:) gives a level’s communities, parent(of:) and children(of:) walk the tree, leaves are the final communities, and community(of:) gives a person’s final community.

Where it breaksThe cap decides what you see

Set the cap to 24 in the lab and there is one level: whatever Leiden finds first. Set it to 3 and the teams of four are over the cap, but a team of four is a clique, and Leiden will not split a clique, so they stay final at four. The cap is a request, not a promise.

Leiden is randomised, so two runs can split a borderline community differently. The lab uses a fixed seed. For stable results in your own code, pass a seeded generator, as the code panel does.
