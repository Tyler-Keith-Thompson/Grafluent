Label propagation

If everyone repeats whatever most of their friends say, which groups end up saying the same thing?

What it’s forFast groups in a very large network

Label propagation splits a network into groups, called communities, that have many links inside and few between. Each vertex copies the label most of its neighbours have. Inside a close group one label soon wins, and it rarely crosses the few links to the next group.

Reach for it when the network is huge and you want a quick first look. It never scores a split, so it is very fast. It also has no idea of a best answer, so check what it finds with a score such as modularity.

Compared withOther ways to find communities

Label propagation is the fastest of the three. It is also the least steady. The order of the visits and the way ties break change its answer. In the table, n is the number of vertices and m the number of links.

Example: sixteen children on a playground, and who plays with whom. Each child starts with a rumour of their own. Then each keeps switching to the rumour most of their friends tell. When nobody wants to switch, the children who tell the same rumour are a group.

The ideaA local rule, and the order of turns

Each child looks only at their own friends. A sweep visits every child once, so it looks at every friendship about twice. A handful of sweeps is usually enough, which is why the method scales to networks with millions of vertices.

The order matters. asynchronousLabelPropagationCommunities() visits one child at a time. Later children hear the changes earlier ones made in the same sweep. If everyone switched at the same moment, two friends could swap rumours with each other for ever. So labelPropagationCommunities() works in rounds. In each round only children who aren’t friends switch together. Those rounds come from colouring the network so that no two friends share a colour. Each colour switches together, and this way always settles.

Where it breaksTies, and one rumour for everyone

At the start every rumour has exactly one teller, so ties are everywhere. The tie rule and the order decide a lot. Grafluent’s in-order form takes the last label in the list on a tie. That makes it repeatable, but the choice is arbitrary. The random form breaks ties by chance, so two seeds can give two answers, as Draw again shows.

On a network without clear groups, one rumour can flood everything and leave a single group. The same happens when groups are joined by many links. Use label propagation for a fast first look, and score the result with modularity before you trust it.
