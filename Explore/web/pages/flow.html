Maximum flow

How much water can a network of pipes carry from the reservoir to the town?

What it’s forThe most a network can carry, and its bottleneck

A maximum flow is the most that can travel from one place, the source, to another, the sink. Each link can carry only so much. The most a link can carry is its capacity. The answer also comes with a minimum cut. That is the set of full links that holds the flow back, so it shows what to widen first.

Reach for it with pipes, roads, data links or anything else with a limit per link. Many other problems turn into a flow, such as pairing people with jobs or splitting an image into foreground and background.

Compared withOther ways to find a maximum flow

All four give the same amount of flow and the same minimum cut. Edmonds–Karp is the easiest to follow, which is why this page uses it. For real work, start with maximumFlow. In the table, n is the number of places and m the number of pipes.

Example: a reservoir, a town, two pumps, a junction and a tank, joined by one-way pipes. Each pipe shows the water it carries over the most it can carry.

The ideaSend what fits, and let water flow backwards

Find a route from the reservoir to the town where every pipe still has room. Send as much as its tightest pipe allows. Then repeat.

That alone can get stuck after a poor first choice. Here the first route uses the pipe from the North pump to the Junction, and the South pump needed that pipe. The fix is to let a route run backwards along a pipe that already carries water. That cancels some of the water, and the North pump sends it another way. The room left in each pipe, plus these backward options, is the residual network.

When no route through the residual network reaches the town, the flow is as large as it can be. The places the reservoir can still reach are cut off from the rest by full pipes. Every route to the town crosses one of them. So no flow can be larger than their total, and this flow equals it. That is the max-flow min-cut theorem.

Edmonds–Karp always picks a route with the fewest pipes, found by breadth-first search. That rule keeps the work at O(nm²), whatever the capacities are.

Where it breaksPick routes carelessly and it crawls

Without the fewest-pipes rule the method is Ford and Fulkerson’s. A bad choice of routes can then need as many rounds as the flow is large. With capacities like √2 it may never finish.

When each pipe also has a cost per unit, the question changes. You want the cheapest way to send a given amount. That is a minimum-cost flow.
