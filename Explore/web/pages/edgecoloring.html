Edge coloring

Say Vizingvee-ZING/viˈzinɡ/

Every team plays every other once, and nobody plays twice on the same day. How few days does the tournament need?

What it’s forRounds where nobody is busy twice

An edge colouring gives every line a colour, so that the lines at any one vertex all differ. Each colour is then a set of lines with no vertex in common. In a league, a line is a match and a colour is a round. No team plays twice in one round.

Reach for it to schedule anything that pairs things up, such as matches, meetings between two people, or transfers between machines on a network. Each round can then run all at once.

Compared withOther ways to colour the lines

Δ is the most lines at any one vertex, here the most matches any team plays. No method can use fewer than Δ colours. edgeColoring() works on any simple graph and is never more than one colour off. In the table, n is the number of teams and m the number of matches.

Example: a small league where each team plays each other team once. A line is a match. A round is a set of matches with no team in two of them.

The ideaOnly two possible answers

A team with Δ matches needs Δ different rounds, so Δ is a lower bound. Vizing proved in 1964 that Δ + 1 is always enough for a simple graph. So there are only two possible answers. The fewest colours is the graph’s chromatic index.

edgeColoring() always reaches one of the two. It runs Misra and Gries’s version of Vizing’s proof. It colours the matches one by one. When a match is stuck, it shifts colours round the matches at one of its teams, in turn, and swaps two colours along a path, until a colour is free.

For the league, Δ is the number of other teams, n − 1. With an even number of teams, n − 1 rounds are possible. The circle method seats the teams round a table and keeps one seat fixed. Each round the others move one place, and teams in facing seats play. With an odd number of teams, each round leaves one team out, so n rounds are needed. The circle method adds an empty seat for the team that rests. isEdgeColoring(_:) checks any colouring you bring.

Where it breaksΔ or Δ + 1 is a hard question

Vizing’s method promises Δ + 1. Sometimes it gives Δ + 1 when Δ would do. Pick an even number of teams and compare its schedule with the circle method’s. That isn’t carelessness. Deciding whether a graph needs Δ or Δ + 1 colours is NP-hard, so no fast method always finds the smaller. On a bipartite graph, such as home teams against visiting teams, Kőnig’s theorem, a different result by the same Kőnig, says Δ is always enough.

Real fixtures have more rules than this, such as home and away games, travel and rest days. Those are usually solved with general-purpose solvers, starting from a colouring like this one.
