Resilience

A data centre has twelve racks and twenty patches (racks are the vertices, patch cables the links). How many can fail at once before some rack is cut off, and which racks hold together best?

What it’s forHow much failure a network takes

A network’s vertex connectivity is the fewest vertices whose failure splits it; its edge connectivity is the fewest links. A cut is a set whose removal splits the network, and a minimum vertex cut is a smallest one. The k-components refine this inside the network: the largest groups that stay connected through any k − 1 failures among their own members, for every k; a 3-component survives any two failures.

Reach for them to rate a network’s robustness, to find where to add a link so one failure no longer isolates a site, or to find the tightly knit cores of a social network, which is what the sociologists Moody and White built k-components for.

Compared withOther ways to find weak points and tight groups

Bridges and articulation points answer the k = 1 question fast. Connectivity and k-components answer it for every k, at a price that grows with the network. In the table, n is the number of racks and m the number of patches.

Example: two rings of four racks, each fully cross-linked, joined through two patch panels, with a lab and a desk looped through one ring.

Step throughThe groups at each k

The ideaCount the disjoint paths

The fewest vertices whose failure separates two others equals the most paths between them that share no vertex. That is Menger’s theorem. So a flow algorithm (see Maximum flow) counts these paths, by letting each vertex carry only one path. vertexConnectivity() takes the least over the pairs it must check, and minimumVertexCut() reads the cut off the flow. edgeConnectivity() does the same, letting each link carry only one path.

kComponents() builds the hierarchy by finding the connectivity k of a piece, cutting it at a minimum cut, and continuing in each part until it falls apart. Every group at k lies inside a group at k − 1, and the 2-components are the blocks with three or more vertices. In the centre, each ring of four is a 3-component, because four racks with every pair linked form a complete graph, K₄, and survive any two failures. The two panels join the rings into a 2-component. The lab and the desk, in a loop through two racks of one ring, belong to the 2-component but to no 3-component.

Where it breaksA flow per pair adds up

Each connectivity question is a maximum flow, and k-components ask many of them, so on a network of thousands of vertices they take a while and on millions they are impractical. For a single point of failure use bridges and articulation points instead, which cost one pass. The numbers also say nothing about likelihood: a cut of two racks that share a power supply is worse than its size suggests.
