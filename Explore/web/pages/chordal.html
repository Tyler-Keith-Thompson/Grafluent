Chordal graphs

Does every loop of four or more friends have a shortcut through it?

Example: eight friends. Go round a loop of friends, each friends with the next. A chord is a friendship between two of them who aren’t next to each other on the loop, a shortcut across it. A graph is chordal when every loop of four or more has one; a loop without one is a hole.

The ideaTaking people away one at a time

  
Checking every loop would take forever: there can be exponentially many. Chordal graphs have another description that is quick to check. A vertex is simplicial when its neighbours all know each other. Every chordal graph has one; take it away and what is left is still chordal, so it has another, and so on. The order this gives is a perfect elimination ordering, and a graph has one exactly when it is chordal.

  
Finding the order is the clever part. Tarjan and Yannakakis showed that a maximum cardinality search, always visiting next the vertex with the most visited neighbours, visits a chordal graph in the reverse of a perfect elimination ordering. So one search and one check decide the question in time proportional to vertices plus edges: isChordal. Rose, Tarjan and Lueker’s lexicographic breadth-first search, lexicographicBreadthFirstSearch(), works the same way with a finer tie-break, and is the starting point for recognising other families of graphs.

  
Chordal graphs are worth recognising because hard problems turn easy on them. Following a perfect elimination ordering gives the largest clique, a colouring with the fewest colours and the largest independent set, each in linear time. They come up in practice too: sparse matrix factorisation adds “fill-in” edges to make a matrix’s graph chordal, and interval graphs, the overlaps of time slots, are always chordal.


  
Where it breaksA hole is the proof

  
When the test fails, it says no but not why. findHole() supplies the why: a loop of four or more with no shortcut, a witness anyone can check by eye. Finding it is slower than the yes-or-no test, since it searches around the vertices where the test failed.

  
Making a graph chordal by adding the fewest edges, the minimum fill-in problem, is NP-hard. Any elimination order gives some chordal completion, which is why sparse solvers spend effort choosing a good order rather than the best one.
