k shortest paths

The quickest route is blocked. What’s the next best way across town, and the one after that?

What it’s forBackup routes, best first

When the best route is blocked, you want the next best, and then the one after that. A k shortest paths search lists the routes from one place to another, quickest first, as many as you ask for. No route visits a place twice.

Reach for it to offer a driver some alternatives, or to plan a backup link in a network. It also helps when the best route must pass an extra test, such as avoiding tolls. List the routes in order, and stop at the first one that passes.

Compared withOther ways to find routes

A k shortest paths search gives the true ranking, and it does no work for routes you never ask for. It gets slow when you ask for thousands of routes on a large map.

Example: nine places in a small town, with driving minutes on each road. The routes from Home to the Stadium come out one at a time, quickest first.

The ideaEvery new route is a detour off an old one

The quickest route is one Dijkstra search. For the second, notice that any other route agrees with the first for a while, maybe only at Home, and then turns off somewhere. So try each stop of the first route as the turning point. Keep the stretch before it, and forbid the road the first route took from there. Then search for the quickest way to finish without going back through the kept stretch. Each turning point gives one candidate. The cheapest candidate is the second route.

Yen’s algorithm repeats this. After route k is found, it tries every turning point along route k. At each one it forbids the next road of every earlier route that shares the same opening, so no route can come out twice. Candidates wait in a priority queue, and the cheapest one is always the next route. Lawler’s refinement skips the turning points before the place where route k itself turned off, since those were tried already. On the map, the blue line is the earlier route that a new one shares its opening with, and the yellow stretch is the detour.

In Grafluent, shortestSimplePaths(from:to:weight:) returns a lazy sequence. Nothing past the first route is searched until you ask for it, so prefix(k) costs only what k routes cost. Without a weight, it counts roads instead of minutes.

Where it breaksNear-copies, and a search per stop

The next quickest route is usually the quickest with one small wiggle, such as a block around the corner and back. A route planner wants alternatives that differ by more than that. So it filters the list, for example dropping routes that share most of their roads with one already shown. Or it makes roads already used cost more and searches again. Yen gives the true ranking. Deciding which alternatives are worth showing is a separate choice.

Each new route costs up to one shortest-path search per stop on the route before it, so asking for thousands of routes on a large map is slow. Weights must not be negative, because the searches are Dijkstra’s. And the routes never visit a place twice. When repeats are allowed, Eppstein’s algorithm lists the k shortest walks much faster.
