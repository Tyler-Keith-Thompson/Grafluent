Minimum-cost flow

Two warehouses, four shops, roads that carry only so much and cost so much per crate. What’s the cheapest way to stock every shop?

Example: North has 6 crates and South 5. They reach four shops directly or through a hub. Each road is labelled with the crates sent over the crates it can take, then the cost per crate.

The ideaSupplies, demands, capacities and costs

  
A minimum-cost flow problem gives each place a supply (positive: it sends that much more than it receives) or a demand (negative), and each edge a capacity and a cost per unit. The answer is the flow that meets every supply and demand, never exceeds a capacity, and costs least in total. Shortest paths, maximum flow and the assignment problem are all special cases.

  
The page uses a standard trick to let the warehouses send up to their stock rather than exactly it: one made-up place, Stock, supplies everything wanted, with a free road to each warehouse as wide as its stock. Then each shop’s demand is its order, and the solver decides which warehouse fills it.

  
Cheapest isn’t greedy. Ashby is cheapest to stock through the hub, until Colby opens: Colby’s only cheap road starts at the hub, and the hub can take in only 6 crates a day. So the cheapest plan for three shops moves Ashby’s crates to the dearer direct road, freeing the hub for Colby. minimumCostFlow runs the network simplex: it keeps a spanning tree of roads whose flows are free to change, and repeatedly brings in a road that would lower the cost, sending flow round the cycle it closes, until no road can. The answer comes with a price, a potential, at every place; no road with room left is cheaper than the difference in price across it, which proves no better plan exists.


  
Where it breaksWhole crates, straight costs

  
Costs here grow in a straight line: two crates cost twice one. Real deliveries have a fixed cost per truck, discounts for bulk, or a choice of whether to open a warehouse at all, and those make the problem much harder: they need integer programming, with a flow problem like this one inside. When every supply, demand and capacity is a whole number, the network simplex always finds a plan in whole crates, which is why it suits goods that don’t split.

  
If the supplies can’t all be met, because the shops want more than the roads can carry, there is no flow and the call returns nil. To send as much as possible and then pay least for it, use minimumCostMaximumFlow.
