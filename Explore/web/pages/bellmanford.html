Bellman–Ford

Is it better to change dollars into francs through euros or through pounds? And can a round of trades ever end with more money than it started with?

What it’s forCheapest routes when some costs are negative

Bellman–Ford finds the cheapest route from one starting place to every other place. It still works when some steps have a negative cost, meaning the step pays you back. It also notices a loop that gets cheaper every time you go round it. Then no cheapest route exists, and Bellman–Ford hands you the loop.

Reach for it when costs can be negative, as with exchange rates, rebates, or the energy an electric car gets back going downhill. If no cost is negative, use Dijkstra’s algorithm instead. It is much faster.

Compared withOther ways to find cheapest routes

Bellman–Ford is the safe choice when costs can be negative, and it can show you a loop that pays. It is slow, because each round may check every step again. In the table, n is the number of places and m the number of steps between them.

The ideaRelax every trade, again and again

Trading along a chain multiplies the rates, and the best chain has the largest product. Logarithms turn products into sums. So give each trade the cost −ln(rate), and the best rate becomes the cheapest path. A rate above 1 gets a negative cost.

Negative costs are what break Dijkstra’s algorithm. In this market it would settle francs at 0.864, by way of euros, before it looked at pounds. The route through pounds gives 0.8736, but Dijkstra never goes back to a currency it has settled.

Bellman–Ford never settles anything early. It keeps a best cost for every currency. Each round, it tries the trades out of every currency that improved in the round before. When a trade gives a cheaper way to its target, the target’s best cost drops. This is called relaxing the trade.

After round k, every route of up to k trades has been tried. A cheapest route visits each place at most once, so n − 1 rounds are always enough. If something still improves in round n, some loop has a negative total cost. That is a negative cycle, and in a market it is an arbitrage. lazy.bellmanFord(from:weight:) reports each round and each trade, and at the end the cycle itself.

Where it breaksSlow, and real markets

Each round can touch every trade, so the worst case is O(nm), far slower than Dijkstra. Grafluent only rescans the currencies that changed, which helps a great deal in practice. On a graph without cycles, the topological-order method needs only one pass.

Real arbitrage loops are tiny and gone in moments, and trading fees usually eat them. The costs here are rounded to whole numbers. That works only because this market’s loop clears the rounding by a wide margin.
