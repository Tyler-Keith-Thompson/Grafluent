Covers and dominating sets

Where should the cameras go so every street is watched, and the fire stations so every corner is close to one?

Example: twelve street corners. A camera on a corner sees every street that meets there; corners with cameras that see every street form a vertex cover. A fire station serves its corner and every corner one street away; corners whose stations serve everywhere form a dominating set.

The ideaEasy to check, hard to make small

  
Checking a plan is quick: isVertexCover(_:) looks at every street once, and isDominatingSet(_:) at every corner and its neighbours. Finding the smallest plan is another matter. Both problems are NP-hard, so minimumVertexCover() and minimumDominatingSet() search, using rules that settle easy parts first, such as a corner on a dead end: its one street is best watched from the other end. They answer in a moment for a town this size; the time can grow exponentially for large, tangled networks.

  
Many plans can be equally small. minimumVertexCover() returns whichever its search finds first. The lexicographicallyFirst spellings fix the choice by a rule, the plan whose earliest-listed corner comes first, so the answer depends only on the town and the order its corners are listed, which matters when a result is saved, compared or tested.

  
When the exact search is too slow, the approximations are quick. approximateMinimumVertexCover() goes through the streets and, for each one still unwatched, puts a camera at an end: never more than twice as many as needed. approximateMinimumDominatingSet() is the greedy plan in the second lab, Chvátal’s: always the corner that serves the most corners not yet served, never worse than about the logarithm of the busiest corner’s streets times the best.


  
Where it breaksSmall isn’t always best

  
The fewest cameras may still be the wrong plan if corners differ in cost: a camera on a quiet lane is cheaper than one on a busy junction. Both approximations take a weight: for that. A dominating set also says nothing about distance beyond one street, or about how many corners each station serves; a station on a corner with eight streets may be swamped while another serves one. Those are different problems, closer to the facility location and k-centre problems.

  
Covers and independent sets are two sides of one coin: the corners without cameras never share a street, so a smallest cover is the complement of a largest independent set. On a two-sided graph, such as streets joining north-side and south-side corners only, Kőnig’s theorem makes the cover as easy as a matching.
