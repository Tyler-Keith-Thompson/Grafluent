What is the shortest round trip that visits every town once and comes home?

Example: eleven towns, every pair joined by a straight road. There are 1,814,400 different round trips through them. Quick rules of thumb get close to the shortest; finding the shortest itself gets out of hand fast as towns are added.

The ideaA good tour fast, then a better one

  
Nearest neighbour is what most people do by hand: always go to the closest town you haven’t been to. It takes a moment, and it usually lands within a quarter or so of the shortest. Its weakness is the end, when the only towns left are far apart and the road home is long.

  
2-opt repairs a tour. Two roads that cross can always be swapped for two that don’t, and the swap is shorter: take two roads A–B and C–D, replace them with A–C and B–D, and turn the stretch from B to C around so the tour still joins up. Repeat while any swap saves distance. The result has no crossings and is usually within a few percent of the shortest. Simulated annealing makes random changes instead, and sometimes accepts one that makes the tour longer, less often as it “cools”, so it can get out of a tour that no single change improves.

  
Christofides’ method comes with a promise: on straight-line distances it is never more than one and a half times the shortest. It joins the towns by a minimum spanning tree, pairs up the towns where the tree has an odd number of roads, walks the result as an Euler circuit, and skips towns it has already seen. The shortest tour itself comes from shortestTour(weight:), Held and Karp’s dynamic programming: the best way to visit each subset of towns ending at each town, built up from smaller subsets.


  
Where it breaksTwice as many towns, a thousand times the work

  
Held–Karp’s table has an entry for every subset of towns, so each town added doubles it. Eleven towns are instant; twenty need tens of megabytes; thirty would need more memory than any computer has. No known method finds the shortest tour quickly for every map: the problem is NP-hard. That is why the heuristics matter, and why the good ones, run on thousands of towns, are judged by how close they get rather than by whether they are exact.

  
The heuristics also lean on the map being sensible. Christofides’ promise needs the triangle inequality, that going direct is never longer than going through a third town. With one-way streets, tolls or ferries that break it, the guarantee is gone, though the tour is still a tour.
