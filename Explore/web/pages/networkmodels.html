Network growth models

Say itBAH-rah-bah-shee/ˈbɒrɒbaːʃi/

Why do real networks have a few huge hubs, and why is everyone only a few handshakes from everyone else?

What it’s forTest networks with a known shape

A network model is a recipe for making random networks that share one feature with real ones. One recipe makes hubs. Another makes a small world, where friends cluster and yet everyone is close. You use them to test an algorithm on networks that look real, or to check whether a real network’s shape could arise by chance.

Compared withOther random networks

Each model explains one feature of real networks and misses others. Pick the one whose feature you want to test.

Example: two small random networks, each drawn from a seed. The same seed always gives the same network. The first grows one vertex at a time.

The ideaThe rich get richer

In the Barabási–Albert model, a network grows one vertex at a time. Each newcomer links to m vertices already there. It picks each one in proportion to its degree. So a vertex with ten links is ten times as likely to gain the next link as a vertex with one.

Early vertices get a head start, and the advantage grows. A few become hubs, while most stay small. The degrees follow a power law. There are roughly eight times fewer vertices of degree 2d than of degree d. Links placed at random never give a tail that long. Citations, links between web pages and airline routes look like this.

barabasiAlbert starts from a star of m + 1 vertices and makes exactly m(n − m) links.

The second network is a ring where everyone knows their nearest neighbours. Step by step, each link gets a growing chance of moving to someone at random.

The ideaA few shortcuts make a small world

The Watts–Strogatz model starts from a ring. Each vertex knows its k nearest neighbours. Two of your friends are probably friends with each other. This is called high clustering, and averageClustering() measures it. But getting across the ring takes many steps.

Now move each link with chance p, sending one end to a random vertex. A handful of these shortcuts cut the average distance sharply, because each one helps everyone near either end. The clustering hardly changes, since most triangles survive. In between lies the small world. It is close-knit locally and still only a few steps across, like a network of acquaintances.

Where it breaksModels, not maps

Real networks have hubs and clustering both, and more besides. They have communities, vertices that leave, and links that age. Neither model has all of that.

Fitting a power law to real degree data is also easy to get wrong. A tail that looks straight on a log plot is weak evidence. Use the models as a baseline to test ideas against, as with random graphs. Don’t treat them as a description of a real network.
