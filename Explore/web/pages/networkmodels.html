Network growth models

Say itBAH-rah-bah-shee/ˈbɒrɒbaːʃi/

Why do real networks have a few huge hubs, and why is everyone only a few handshakes from everyone else?

Example: two small random networks, each from a seed, so the same seed always gives the same network. First, one that grows a vertex at a time.

The ideaThe rich get richer

  
In the Barabási–Albert model, a network grows one vertex at a time, and each newcomer links to m vertices already there, picking each in proportion to its degree. A vertex with ten links is ten times as likely to gain the next one as a vertex with one. Early vertices get a head start, and the advantage compounds: a few become hubs while most stay small. The degrees follow a power law, roughly as many vertices of degree 2d as of degree d divided by eight, a long tail no network with uniformly random links has. Citations, links between web pages and airline routes look like this.

The ideaA few shortcuts make a small world

  
The Watts–Strogatz model starts from a ring in which each vertex knows its k nearest neighbours. That ring is cliquish: two of your friends are probably friends (high clustering, from averageClustering()). But getting across it takes many steps. Now rewire each link with probability p, moving one end to a random vertex. A handful of shortcuts slash the average distance, because each one serves everyone near either end, while hardly touching the clustering, since most triangles survive. In between lies the small world: close-knit locally, and still only a few steps across, like a network of acquaintances.


  
Where it breaksModels, not maps

  
Each model explains one feature and misses others. Barabási–Albert networks have hubs but little clustering, and every vertex has at least m links; Watts–Strogatz networks are clustered and small but have no hubs, every degree staying near k. Real networks have both, and more: communities, vertices that leave, links that age. Fitting a power law to real degree data is also easy to get wrong; a straight-looking tail on a log plot is weak evidence. Use the models to test ideas against a baseline, as with random graphs, rather than as a description of a real network.
