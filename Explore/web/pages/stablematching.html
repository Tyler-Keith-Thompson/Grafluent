Stable matching

Each student ranks the tutors and each tutor ranks the students. How do you pair them so that no student and tutor would both rather be together?

What it’s forPairs that nobody wants to break

Two sides rank each other. A pairing is unstable if some student and tutor who aren’t together both prefer each other to the partners they have. Those two would break away. A stable matching has no such pair. Gale and Shapley showed in 1962 that one always exists, and gave a fast way to find it.

Reach for it when both sides have a say, as with doctors and hospitals, students and schools, or people and flats. Medical residency matches run this way.

Compared withOther ways to pair two sides

Gale–Shapley respects both sides’ wishes, and nobody has a reason to break a pair. It gives no thought to the total good of the pairing. In the table, n is the number of people on each side and m the number of possible pairs.

Example: four students and four tutors, each with a full ranking of the other side. Switch who asks to see a different answer that is just as stable.

The ideaAsk, hold, trade up

The method is called deferred acceptance. Every free member of the asking side asks the best partner who hasn’t turned them down yet. Whoever is asked holds on to the best offer so far and turns the rest down. When someone better asks, they trade up. Nobody accepts for good until the end.

Each asker works down their list and never asks anyone twice. So it ends after at most n² offers. And the result is stable. Say a student prefers some tutor to their final partner. Then the student asked that tutor first and was turned down. That only happens when the tutor held someone they liked more.

Who asks matters. When students ask, every student gets the best tutor they could have in any stable matching. Every tutor gets the worst. Swap the sides and the reverse holds. Here, with students asking, Ada gets her third choice. With tutors asking, she gets her last. That is why residency matches let the applicants ask.

Where it breaksLying, ties and roommates

The asking side can never gain by lying about its rankings. The other side sometimes can.

With ties in the rankings, or lists that leave some people out, stable matchings can differ in size. Finding the largest is NP-hard.

When there aren’t two sides, as with pairing roommates from one group, a stable matching may not exist at all. Irving’s algorithm finds one when it does.
