The giant component

Say Erdős–RényiAIR-dersh RAY-nyee/ˈɛrdøːʃ ˈreːɲi/

Add friendships at random. When does one group suddenly take in almost everyone?

What it’s forA baseline for any network

A random graph joins pairs of points by chance. It is the simplest network there is. When you study a real network, you compare it with a random one of the same size. Anything the real one does that the random one doesn’t is worth explaining.

The most famous fact about random graphs is the giant component. Below a certain number of links, everyone sits in small groups. Just past it, one group suddenly holds most people. The same jump decides whether a disease can spread through a town, or whether a network stays in one piece when links fail.

Example: fifty people at a new school. Friendships form one at a time, between two people picked at random. A group is everyone joined by a chain of friends. These groups are the connected components.

The ideaA tipping point at one friend each

The Erdős–Rényi random graph G(n, p) has n points. Each pair is joined with chance p, and each pair is decided on its own. A close cousin, G(n, m), picks exactly m links at random. Adding random links one at a time, as above, passes through every G(n, m) in turn. With m links among n people, p is about 2m/n². The average number of friends is c = 2m/n.

Follow a friendship out of a group. On average you meet c new people, and each of them leads on to c more. When c is below 1, that chain dies out fast. Every group stays small, and most are shaped like trees. When c passes 1, the chain can keep going. That is the same as p passing 1/n. Then the many medium groups fuse into one. This giant component holds a fixed share of everyone. It is about 58% at c = 1.5, 80% at c = 2 and 94% at c = 3. No second group ever comes close.

With only fifty people the jump is gentle, and each seed tells a slightly different story. With a million people the curve stays flat near zero right up to p = 1/n. Then it turns sharply upward.

Where it breaksReal networks aren’t coin flips

Real friendships don’t form by coin flips. Real networks have hubs with far more links than average. barabasiAlbert grows those by giving new links mostly to people who are already popular. Real networks are also full of triangles, because friends of friends become friends. wattsStrogatz builds those in. Both models have giant components too, but the jump comes at a different point.

A giant component isn’t the whole graph, either. For everyone to be joined, c has to reach about ln n, the natural logarithm of n. For fifty people that is about 3.9. The school above stops at 3, so a few people are usually still alone at the end.
