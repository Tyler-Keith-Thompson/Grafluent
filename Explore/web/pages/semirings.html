Semirings

Quickest, widest, most reliable: can one route-finding algorithm answer them all?

Example: a small data network. Seven places and eleven one-way links carrying data out from a server, each link with three numbers: its delay in minutes, its bandwidth in megabits a second, and the chance it is up.

In SwiftThe network above, in Grafluent







  
The ideaA route-finder only ever does two things

  
Watch Dijkstra’s algorithm closely and it does only two things with the numbers on the links. It extends a route by one more link: 4 minutes to North, then 3 more to Mill, makes 4 + 3 = 7. And it chooses between two routes to the same place: 7 minutes through North or 4 through South, keep the smaller. Everything else, the queue, the order, the bookkeeping, is about doing those two moves in a sensible order.


  
Swap themAnd the question changes

  
Replace “add” and “keep the smaller” with other pairs and the same algorithm answers a different question. A route’s bandwidth is that of its narrowest link, so extending takes the min, and choosing keeps the wider. A route works only if every link on it is up, so extending multiplies chances, and choosing keeps the likelier.

Each row also needs two special values. No route is what choosing ignores: any real route beats it. Already there is the route of no links, which extending leaves unchanged: adding 0 minutes, or multiplying by a chance of 1.


  
Name itThat pair is a semiring

  
A semiring is exactly such a pair, an extend and a choose, together with those two values, obeying the same rules as × and + do on ordinary numbers. Choosing doesn’t care about order or grouping. Extending can be grouped either way. “No route” changes nothing when chosen, and wipes out a route when extended. And extending distributes over choosing: extending the better of two routes gives the same as choosing the better of the two extensions. That last rule is what lets an algorithm decide at each place once, instead of keeping every route.

  
This is Grafluent’s Semiring protocol: times(_:) extends, plus(_:) chooses, zero is no route and one is already there. TropicalSemiring, BottleneckSemiring, ViterbiSemiring, BooleanSemiring and CountingSemiring are the five rows above, and the code panel changes only which one each link’s value is wrapped in.


  
Where it breaksDijkstra needs two more promises

  
Dijkstra settles a place for good once nothing better can still arrive. That needs extending never to make a route better: no negative minutes, no link wider than infinity, no chance above 1. Grafluent checks each weight against one. It also needs choosing to pick one of the two, not blend them: min, max and or all do. Semirings like that are called selective, Grafluent’s SelectiveSemiring, and that is what dijkstraShortestPaths takes.

  
Counting routes breaks the second promise: choosing adds the two counts, so no single route wins, and a place can’t be settled until every way into it is known. That works when the links can’t loop back, a directed acyclic graph, as here: dagShortestDistances runs Mohri’s algorithm, visiting places in an order where every link points forward. With a loop there would be infinitely many routes to count.
