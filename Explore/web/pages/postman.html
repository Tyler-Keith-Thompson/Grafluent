Chinese postman

A snowplough has to clear every street on the estate and get back to the depot. Which streets should it drive twice, so the whole round is as short as it can be?

Example: seven junctions and nine streets, with the minutes each takes to clear. If every junction had an even number of streets, one Euler circuit would drive each street exactly once. Four of them don’t.

The ideaFix the odd junctions, as cheaply as possible

  
A round that ends where it started leaves every junction as many times as it arrives, so it uses an even number of street-ends at each one. At a junction with an odd number of streets, one street must be driven an extra time. Driving a street twice is the same as adding a copy of it to the map, so the question becomes: which copies, adding up to the fewest minutes, make every junction even?

  
There is always an even number of odd junctions, since every street has two ends. Pair them up, and for each pair, copy the streets on the quickest route between them: that makes both ends even and leaves every junction in between even too. The best answer is the pairing whose routes add up to the least, a minimum-weight perfect matching on the odd junctions with the quickest times between them as weights. With the copies added, an Euler circuit drives the round. This is Edmonds and Johnson’s algorithm, and chinesePostmanTour(weight:) runs it: Dijkstra from each odd junction, the blossom algorithm for the matching, then Hierholzer’s circuit.

  
With four odd junctions there are only three pairings, listed in the first lab. With twenty there are over 650 million, which is why the matching is found by an algorithm and not by listing them.


  
Where it breaksOne-way streets, and only some streets

  
If streets are one-way, the parity argument becomes one of balance: each junction needs as many ways in as out. Grafluent’s chinesePostmanTour on a directed graph solves that with a minimum-cost flow instead of a matching, and it answers nil when some street can’t be reached and left again. Mix one-way and two-way streets, though, and the problem becomes NP-hard, as it does when only some streets need clearing (the rural postman problem). Then the practical answers are heuristics.

  
The shortest round also isn’t always the best one in practice: a real plough also cares about turns, especially U-turns, and about which streets are cleared first. Those are extra costs the graph doesn’t carry.
