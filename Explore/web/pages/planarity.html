Planarity

Say itkoo-rah-TOV-skee/kuraˈtɔfskʲi/

Can three houses each get water, gas and power without any two pipes crossing?

Example: the three-utilities puzzle. Three houses, three utilities, and a pipe from every house to every utility, nine in all, on flat ground. Then five towns with a road between every pair, and a cube, whose usual drawing has crossings it doesn’t need.

The ideaTwo small graphs are behind every failure

  
A graph is planar when it can be drawn on paper with no two edges crossing. The drawing you start with doesn’t matter: the cube looks tangled as first drawn, yet planarLayout() finds it a drawing with none. What matters is whether some drawing exists.

  
The puzzle has no solution. Its graph is K₃,₃: three vertices each joined to the same other three. Five towns all joined to each other make K₅. In 1930 Kuratowski proved these two are the only obstacles: a graph is planar exactly when it contains no copy of either, even one stretched out with extra stops along its paths. So a “no” always comes with a proof you can check by eye, and findKuratowskiSubgraph() returns it, red above.

  
A planar drawing splits the paper into faces, the outside counting as one. For a connected planar graph, Euler’s formula ties them together: vertices − edges + faces = 2. Since every face needs at least three edges round it, a simple planar graph has at most 3n − 6 edges, which already rules out K₅: 10 is more than 3 × 5 − 6 = 9.

  
Testing takes time proportional to the size of the graph. isPlanar and planarEmbedding() run de Fraysseix and Rosenstiehl’s left-right test, built on depth-first search; findKuratowskiSubgraph() runs Boyer and Myrvold’s edge addition, which keeps the proof when it fails.


  
Where it breaksPlanar isn’t the same as easy to read

  
A planar layout avoids crossings but can squeeze vertices close together and stretch edges out, as the cube’s does. For a drawing that shows a graph’s shape, crossings and all, use a force-directed layout. And when a graph isn’t planar, the fewest crossings any drawing can have is a much harder question: there is no fast method for it.

  
Circuit boards and maps care about planarity directly. For many other problems it is a gift: every planar graph can be coloured with four colours, the four-colour theorem, and many hard problems get easier on planar graphs.
