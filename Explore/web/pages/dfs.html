Depth-first search

Starting from one vertex, how do you visit everything you can reach, one corridor at a time?

Example: a small office building. Rooms are vertices, doors are edges, and you start at reception. The doors are one-way, like fire doors that only open from one side, so the graph is directed.

When to use itStructure, not distance

Depth-first search finds everything you can reach from a start, like breadth-first search. Its order is what makes it useful. The times it enters and leaves each room show whether the graph has a cycle and how its parts hang together. Reach for it to find cycles, to put tasks in order, or to split a graph into its strongly connected parts.

The ideaAs deep as it goes, then back up

Depth-first search follows one corridor as far as it goes. Then it backs up to the last junction and tries the next way. It is the natural way to explore a maze. It keeps a stack of the rooms it has entered but not yet finished. The newest one is always next. The stack is the path from reception to where you stand.

The search gives every door it meets one of four kinds.

  • A tree edge leads to a new room.
  • A back edge leads to a room still on the current path. It is the sign of a cycle.
  • A forward edge leads to a room already finished, further along the same corridor.
  • A cross edge leads into a branch that is already finished.

Topological sort and strongly connected components are both built on these kinds and times.

Where it breaksDeep is not near

The first route depth-first search finds to a room can be a long way round. For the route with the fewest doors, use breadth-first search. When doors have different lengths, use Dijkstra’s algorithm.
