Which single road closure splits the town?

Example: a village of nine places and ten two-way roads. A road whose closure cuts the village in two is a bridge; a place whose closure does the same is an articulation point, or cut vertex.

The ideaCan you get back without the road you came by?

  
Trying every closure in turn means one connectivity check per road: slow on a big network. A single depth-first search answers for every road at once. The search numbers each place by when it first arrives there, its time. The roads it walks to new places form a tree; every other road leads back to a place already on the current path, a back road.

  
Each place also keeps a low-link: the earliest time that it, or anything the search reached from it, can get back to with one back road. When the search leaves a place, its low-link is final, and one comparison with its parent decides everything:

  
    
    
  • If the low-link is later than the parent’s time, nothing below gets back to the parent except along the tree road between them. That road is a bridge.
    • 
    
  • If it is no earlier than the parent’s time, the branch below can’t get above the parent, so the parent is an articulation point. The start is the exception: it is one when the search had to set out from it more than once.
    • 
  

  
Each road is looked at once from each end, so the whole thing takes time proportional to the number of places plus roads. Grafluent’s bridges() and articulationPoints() run this search over flat rows; the timeline above rebuilds the times and low-links from lazy.edgeDepthFirstSearch’s events.


  
Where it breaksLoops have no bridges, and that’s the point

  
Inside the loop of Mill, Church, Square and School, or the triangle of Station, Market and Harbour, no single closure cuts anyone off: there is always another way round. Such a part is two-edge-connected, and biEdgeConnectedComponents() lists them. So the cure for a fragile network is a loop: one new road from Farm to Church, and Mill–Farm stops being a bridge.

  
Places are a different question from roads. Two triangles that share one corner have no bridge at all, yet taking away the shared corner splits them. Each triangle is a block, a biconnected component, and the corner is in both.

  
  
For a network that must survive any one failure, ask for no articulation point: isBiconnected. For any two failures, the question becomes one of flow: how many separate routes join each pair.
