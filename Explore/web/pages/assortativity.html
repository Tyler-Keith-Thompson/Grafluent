Assortativity

In a newsroom the editors all talk to each other. In an airline the small airports all fly to one hub. Which network is which, from the numbers alone?

What it’s forWhether like links to like

A network is assortative when vertices with many links tend to link to other vertices with many links, and disassortative when the busy ones link to the quiet ones. The degree assortativity coefficient puts a number on it, from −1 to 1, like a correlation.

Reach for it to tell a social network, where popular people know each other, from a technological one, where hubs serve leaves. It also says how a network will fail: an assortative network keeps a tough core when its edge frays, a disassortative one falls apart when a hub goes.

Compared withOther ways to read the mixing

The coefficient is the summary. The per-vertex and per-degree numbers show the shape behind it.

Example: a newsroom of three editors, three reporters and three juniors, and an airline of one hub and eight small airports.

Step throughEach vertex against its neighbours

The ideaCorrelate the two ends of every link

Take every link and write down the degrees of its two ends. The assortativity coefficient is the correlation of those pairs, the same formula as for any two columns of numbers. In the newsroom the editors’ links join degree 3 to degree 3, each reporter’s link to an editor joins 2 to 3, and each junior’s link joins 1 to 2: the busy sit with the busy and the quiet with the quiet, so the coefficient is positive. In the airline every link to the hub joins 8 to 1 or 2, so the coefficient is strongly negative. degreeAssortativityCoefficient() computes it, and returns nil when every degree is the same, since a constant has no correlation.

averageNeighborDegrees() gives each vertex the mean degree of its neighbours; the lab shows that number as “near”. averageDegreeConnectivity() groups those means by the vertex’s own degree. In an assortative network the means rise with the degree; in a disassortative one they fall.

Where it breaksOne number for a whole network

The coefficient is a correlation, so it is pulled by whatever is common. A network with one huge hub and many leaves is disassortative however the rest is wired. And a network can be assortative in one region and disassortative in another, which one number cannot show; the per-degree averages can. Directed networks have four coefficients, one for each choice of in- or out-degree at each end, and averageNeighborDegrees(source:target:) picks them.
