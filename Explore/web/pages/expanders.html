Expanders

Twenty-nine machines, and each may have three cables at most. Can a message from one reach all of them in six hops?

What it’s forWell connected with few cables

An expander is a graph with few links per vertex (sparse) that behaves like one where nearly everything is linked (dense). Every vertex has only a few neighbours, yet any group you pick has many links out to the rest, so there are no bottlenecks and nothing is far from anything else. A single number computed from the graph’s links, the spectral gap, measures this. Bigger means fewer bottlenecks and a better expander; the Spectral drawing page has the maths.

Reach for one when you need a network that is cheap to build and hard to cut: the switches of a data centre, or the machines of a peer-to-peer network. A random wander through an expander also spreads out fast, which is why random sampling leans on them.

Compared withOther ways to wire a few cables each

The ring is the cheapest wiring and the slowest. The complete graph is the fastest and the dearest. An expander takes a few more cables than a ring and gets most of the way to the complete graph. In the table, n is the number of machines and d the cables each has.

Example: twenty-nine machines in a ring, wired as a plain ring, as a ring where each machine also reaches the one two along, and as the chordal cycle, a ring with one chord, an extra cable across the ring, from each machine to its inverse modulo 29, which is explained below.

The ideaChords from arithmetic

The chordal cycle on a prime p is the ring 0, 1, …, p − 1 with a chord from each machine x to the machine x⁻¹, the number that gives 1 when multiplied by x modulo p. Only 1 and p − 1 are their own inverses, so they get no chord, and every machine has two or three cables. Those chords look random but are not, and Lubotzky showed the family expands. As p grows, the gap never shrinks towards zero, however big the ring. In the lab the chordal cycle on 29 machines uses 40 cables and reaches everyone in six hops; the plain ring, with 29 cables, needs fourteen, and the ring with a skip, with 58, still needs seven.

Two more families come from the same kind of arithmetic. margulisGabberGalil(side:) puts n² machines on a grid and joins (x, y) to (x + 2y, y), (x + 2y + 1, y), (x, y + 2x) and (x, y + 2x + 1), all modulo n: at most eight cables each, and a spectral gap bounded away from zero. paley(prime:) joins x and y when y − x is a nonzero square modulo p, for a prime p that leaves remainder 1 when divided by 4. Every machine has (p − 1)/2 cables, and the graph is its own complement, the graph of the links it doesn’t have. The lab reads the gap with fiedlerVector(), whose algebraicConnectivity is that second eigenvalue, and counts the hops with breadthFirstLayers(from:).

Where it breaksThe sizes are fixed

The chordal cycle and the Paley graph need a prime number of vertices, and Margulis–Gabber–Galil a square; for other sizes randomRegular(vertexCount:degree:) gives an expander with high probability but no guarantee. Built as simple graphs, these families drop self-loops and merge parallel edges, so the number of cables per machine is a maximum, not exact: a chordal cycle has some machines with two cables, a Margulis–Gabber–Galil graph some with four. And a large gap says the network has no bottleneck, not that it is fast: the hops still count, and on 29 machines the best is six.
