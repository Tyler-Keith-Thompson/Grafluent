Spectral drawing and the Fiedler cut

Say itFEED-ler/ˈfiːdlər/

Can a network’s own numbers say how to draw it, and where to cut it in two?

What it’s forA drawing and a split, from one vector

Spectral methods give every vertex a number taken from an eigenvector of the graph. Vertices that are linked get numbers close together. Lay the vertices out by those numbers and you get a drawing. Cut the line of numbers in two and you get a split with few links across.

Reach for it when you want to split a network into two even halves, such as two teams or two halves of a circuit board, with as few links between them as you can. It needs no random start, so the same network always gives the same answer.

Compared withOther ways to split a network in two

The Fiedler cut keeps the two sides even without being told their sizes. It only ever cuts in two. As a drawing, it is quicker than a force-directed layout and the same every time, but it can pile vertices onto one spot.

Example: thirteen friends in two circles, of six and seven, with a few links between the circles. Write the network as its Laplacian. That is a table with each person’s friend count down the diagonal and −1 for each friendship. Its eigenvectors give every person a number, and those numbers draw the network and find its two halves.

The ideaFriends get nearby numbers

Give each person a number x. For each friendship, take the difference of its two numbers and square it. Add these up. The total is small when friends get similar numbers. The Laplacian L is exactly the table that computes it, since the total is xᵀLx.

Giving everyone the same number makes the total 0, which tells you nothing. So ask for the smallest total among numbers that average 0 and have length 1. The answer is the eigenvector of L’s second smallest eigenvalue. That is the Fiedler vector. Friends end up close together. The two circles, joined by only a few links, end up on opposite sides of 0.

The eigenvalue itself, λ₂, is the algebraic connectivity. It is 0 exactly when the network is in pieces. It grows as the pieces are joined more strongly, which the first drawing shows as you open and close the links. spectralLayout() uses the Fiedler vector across and the next eigenvector down. So the drawing comes from the network alone, with no randomness and no forces to settle.

Cutting at 0 is the simplest split, but any point along the line is a candidate. The sweep tries every one and scores each by the ratio cut. That weighs the links cut against how uneven the two sides are, so cutting off one person isn’t a bargain. Cheeger’s inequality guarantees that the best point on the line is never far from the best of all possible splits.

Where it breaksWhen the network has no obvious seam

On a ring, a grid or a complete graph, λ₂ has several eigenvectors at once, and which one comes out is arbitrary. The drawing and the cut are then one of many equally good answers. A tiny change to the network can change them completely. The method also cuts in two only. For more groups, take more eigenvectors and cluster the points, or use a method made for groups, such as Louvain.

Hubs pull the plain Laplacian around. A person with many friends dominates the totals, and the cut can end up slicing off a few loosely attached people instead of finding the real halves. The normalized Laplacian divides by friend counts to even this out. Ask for it with fiedlerVector(normalized: true).
