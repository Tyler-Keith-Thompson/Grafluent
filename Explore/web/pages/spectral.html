Spectral drawing and the Fiedler cut

Say itFEED-ler/ˈfiːdlər/

Can a network’s own numbers say how to draw it, and where to cut it in two?

Example: thirteen friends in two circles, of six and seven, with a few links between the circles. Write the network as its Laplacian, a table with each person’s friend count down the diagonal and −1 for each friendship. Its eigenvectors give every person a number, and those numbers draw the network and find its two halves.

The ideaFriends get nearby numbers

  
Give each person a number x. For each friendship, take the difference of its two numbers and square it; add these up. That total is small when friends get similar numbers, and the Laplacian L is exactly the table that computes it: the total is xᵀLx. Giving everyone the same number makes it zero, which tells you nothing, so ask for the smallest total among numbers that average 0 and have unit length. The answer is the eigenvector of L’s second smallest eigenvalue: the Fiedler vector. Friends end up close together, and the two circles, joined by only a few links, end up on opposite sides of 0.

  
The eigenvalue itself, λ₂, is the algebraic connectivity. It is 0 exactly when the network is in pieces, and it grows as the pieces are joined more strongly, which the first lab shows as you open and close the links. spectralLayout() uses the Fiedler vector across and the next eigenvector down, so the drawing comes from the network alone, with no randomness and no forces to settle.

  
Cutting at 0 is the simplest use, but any point along the line is a candidate. The sweep tries every one and scores each by the ratio cut, the links cut weighted against how uneven the two sides are, so cutting off one person isn’t a bargain. Cheeger’s inequality guarantees that the best threshold is never far from the best of all possible splits.


  
Where it breaksWhen the network has no obvious seam

  
On a ring, a grid or a complete graph, λ₂ has several eigenvectors at once, and which one comes out is arbitrary. The drawing and the cut are then one of many equally good answers, and they can change completely with a tiny change to the network. The method also cuts in two: for more groups, take more eigenvectors and cluster the points, or use a method made for groups, such as Louvain.

  
Hubs pull the plain Laplacian around. A person with many friends dominates the totals, and the cut can end up slicing off a few loosely attached people instead of finding the real halves. The normalized Laplacian divides by friend counts to even this out; ask for it with fiedlerVector(normalized: true).
