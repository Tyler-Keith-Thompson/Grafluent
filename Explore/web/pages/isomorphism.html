Isomorphism

Say itVICE-fy-ler LAY-mun/ˈvaɪsfaɪlɐ ˈleːman/

Two drawings look nothing alike. Are they the same network, just drawn differently?

Example: three pairs of drawings. Two graphs are isomorphic when the vertices of one can be renamed as the vertices of the other so that exactly the same pairs are joined. Where the vertices sit on the page doesn’t matter.

The ideaRule out quickly, then search

  
Some differences are easy to spot: different numbers of vertices, edges, or vertices of each degree. Colour refinement, the Weisfeiler–Leman test, pushes that idea as far as it goes. Start with every vertex the same colour. Each round, give each vertex a new colour that records its old colour and the multiset of its neighbours’ colours. After one round the colours are the degrees; after two, the degrees of the neighbours too; and so on. If at any round the two graphs have different numbers of vertices of some colour, they can’t be the same graph. weisfeilerLehmanGraphHash() boils the final colours down to one number, and two graphs with different hashes are certainly different. It takes time proportional to the edges, per round.

  
Equal hashes prove nothing, though. To be sure, isomorphism(to:) searches for the renaming: it matches one vertex at a time, trying only vertices that could still correspond, and backs up when an edge doesn’t match. It returns the mapping, or nil when none exists, and isomorphisms(to:) lists every one.


  
Where it breaksGraphs where every vertex looks alike

  
In the look-alikes, every vertex has three neighbours, so every round gives every vertex the same colour: the test sees nothing. Yet one graph has triangles and the other has none, so they are different. Colour refinement can never split a regular graph, one where all degrees are equal, and the cubes pass it for the same reason. The search does the real work on graphs like these.

  
No fast method is known that works on every pair of graphs, and none is known to be impossible either; Babai’s algorithm is quasi-polynomial, and practical searches handle most graphs that come up quickly. For whether a small graph appears inside a bigger one, use subgraphIsomorphisms(to:), which is a harder problem.
