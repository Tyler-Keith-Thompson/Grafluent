Betweenness

Say itBRAN-dess/ˈbrandəs/

When news travels the shortest way through an office, who does it keep passing through?

Example: nine people in an office, and a line between each pair who talk. Two teams, and a few people who talk to both.

The ideaCount the shortest paths through you

  
For every pair of people, look at the shortest chains of conversations between them. If there is one, everyone in its middle gets a point. If there are several equally short ones, the point is split between them: with two shortest paths, each counts a half. A person’s betweenness is their total over all pairs. Someone with a high score is a broker: take them out and many pairs need a longer chain, or can’t reach each other at all.

  
The same count for links is edge betweenness: every shortest path gives its links a share, the pair’s own direct link included. The link with the most is the one most traffic would cross. Removing such links one at a time is how Girvan–Newman splits a network into communities.

  
Counting pair by pair, as the page does, repeats a lot of work. Brandes’ algorithm gets every score from one breadth-first search per person: it counts the shortest paths from that person to everyone, then walks back from the farthest, passing each person’s share to whoever came before them. That is O(nm) for n people and m links, and it is what betweennessCentrality() runs. By default the scores are divided by the number of pairs, so they lie between 0 and 1; normalized: false gives the raw counts shown here.


  
Where it breaksShortest isn’t how everything travels

  
Betweenness assumes things move along shortest paths and nowhere else. Gossip, infections and money often wander, and a person on many second-shortest paths scores nothing. If influence spreads more like a random walk, PageRank or a random-walk betweenness may fit better.

  
On a large network, one search per person is too many. With pivots: or pivotCount:, betweennessCentrality searches from a sample of people and scales up, which estimates the scores well when the sample is random and big enough. With weights, pass weight: and paths are shortest by total weight; ties then need exactly equal sums.
