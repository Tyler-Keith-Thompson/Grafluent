Steiner trees

A company must run cable to three customers across a grid of possible routes. What is the cheapest network that reaches all three?

What it’s forConnecting some places, not all

A minimum spanning tree connects every place. Often you only need some of them, and the rest may help as stepping stones or may be skipped. The cheapest network that connects the chosen places is a Steiner tree, and the places it must reach are its terminals.

Reach for it when you lay cable, pipe or road to a few customers, route a signal to a few pins on a chip, or connect a handful of entities in a knowledge graph with the shortest explanation that joins them.

Compared withOther ways to connect places

With two terminals a Steiner tree is a shortest path. With every place a terminal it is a spanning tree. In between it is a hard problem, and the fast method gets within a factor of two.

Example: eight places, twelve possible cables with their lengths, and a request to connect three of the places.

Step throughLay the cables

The ideaShortest paths between terminals, then a spanning tree of those

Finding the cheapest Steiner tree exactly is NP-hard, so the usual method settles for a good one. Mehlhorn’s version first finds, for every place, its nearest terminal, with one shortest-path search that starts from all the terminals at once. That splits the network into regions, one around each terminal. Then it looks at every cable that crosses from one region into another: such a cable, plus the two paths back to the regions’ terminals, joins two terminals. Take the cheapest set of those joins that connects all the terminals, which is a minimum spanning tree on the terminals, and expand each join into its paths.

The tree it gives costs at most twice the best possible, and in practice much less. steinerTree(terminals:weight:) returns its cables and their total; the lab lays them shortest first so you can watch the tree grow. The last line of the code panel connects every place instead, for comparison.

Where it breaksIt is an approximation

The method can miss a cheaper tree that routes through a place no terminal is nearest to. On small networks you can check it against a search over every possibility; on large ones you accept the bound. If you need the exact answer for a few terminals, a dynamic-programming method over subsets of terminals exists, but its cost doubles with every terminal added.

Weights must not be negative. A place that no cable reaches makes the tree impossible, and the result is nil.
