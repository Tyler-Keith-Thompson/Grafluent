Expanders

Twenty-nine machines, and each may have three cables at most. Can a message from one reach all of them in six hops?

What it’s forWell connected with few cables

An expander is a sparse graph that behaves like a dense one: every vertex has only a few neighbours, yet every set of vertices has many edges leaving it, so there are no bottlenecks and nothing is far from anything else. The spectral gap, the second-smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian, measures this: a bigger gap means a better expander.

Reach for one when you need a network that is cheap to build and hard to cut: the switches of a data centre, the peers of a gossip protocol, or an error-correcting code. Expanders are also what makes random walks mix fast, which is why they turn up in sampling and in derandomization.

The ring is the cheapest wiring and the slowest. The complete graph is the fastest and the dearest. An expander takes a few more cables than a ring and gets most of the way to the complete graph.

The ideaChords from arithmetic

The chordal cycle on a prime p is the ring 0, 1, …, p − 1 with a chord from each machine x to the machine x⁻¹, the number that gives 1 when multiplied by x modulo p. Only 1 and p − 1 are their own inverses, so they get no chord, and every machine has two or three cables. Those chords look random but are not, and Lubotzky showed the family expands: as p grows, the spectral gap stays above a constant. In the lab the chordal cycle on 29 machines uses 40 cables and reaches everyone in six hops; the plain ring, with 29 cables, needs fourteen, and the ring with a skip, with 58, still needs seven.

Two more families come from the same kind of arithmetic. margulisGabberGalil(side:) puts n² machines on a grid and joins (x, y) to (x + 2y, y), (x + 2y + 1, y), (x, y + 2x) and (x, y + 2x + 1), all modulo n: at most eight cables each, and a spectral gap bounded away from zero. paley(prime:) joins x and y when y − x is a nonzero square modulo p, for a prime p ≡ 1 (mod 4): every machine has (p − 1)/2 cables, and the graph is its own complement. The lab reads the gap with fiedlerVector(), whose algebraicConnectivity is that second eigenvalue, and counts the hops with breadthFirstLayers(from:).

Where it breaksThe sizes are fixed

The chordal cycle and the Paley graph need a prime number of vertices, and Margulis–Gabber–Galil a square; for other sizes randomRegular(vertexCount:degree:) gives an expander with high probability but no guarantee. Built as simple graphs, these families drop a step that returns to its own vertex and merge two steps that meet, so the degree is a bound, not a constant: a chordal cycle has some machines with two cables, a Margulis–Gabber–Galil graph some with four. And a large gap says the network has no bottleneck, not that it is fast: the hops still count, and on 29 machines the best is six.

In SwiftThe wirings, in Grafluent

import AdjacencyListModule
import Distances
import GraphProtocols
import NamedGraphs
import SpectralGraphTheory
import Traversal

/// Twenty-nine machines, wired three ways: a ring, a ring where each machine also reaches the one
/// two along, and the chordal cycle, a ring with a chord from each machine to its inverse mod 29.
let machines = 29
let plainRing = UndirectedAdjacencyList<Int>.cycle(vertexCount: machines)
let skipRing = UndirectedAdjacencyList<Int>.circulant(vertexCount: machines, offsets: [1, 2])
let chordRing = UndirectedAdjacencyList<Int>.chordalCycle(prime: machines)

let graph = chordRing

let hops = graph.directedView.breadthFirstLayers(from: 0)  // [[0], [1, 28], [2, 27], [3, 15, 14, 26], [4, 10, 16, 13, 19, 25], [5, 22, 9, 11, 17, 20, 12, 18, 7, 24], [6, 21, 23, 8]]
let cables = graph.edgeCount  // 40
let farthest = graph.diameter()!  // 6
let gap = try graph.fiedlerVector()!.algebraicConnectivity  // 0.30
