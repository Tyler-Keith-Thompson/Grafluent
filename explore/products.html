Graph products

A chess board is four files times three ranks. Which product of two paths gives the rook’s moves, and which the king’s?

What it’s forBig regular graphs from small ones

A product of two graphs has a vertex for every pair of vertices, one from each. Which pairs are joined is the rule that names the product. Grids, tori, hypercubes, boards and the state spaces of several machines running side by side are all products of small graphs.

Reach for a product to build such a graph without listing its edges, to reason about it through its factors, or to model two things that move at once: one step in either factor, or in both.

The Cartesian product keeps the factors apart, so most questions about it reduce to questions about the factors. The strong product is the Cartesian plus the tensor. The lexicographic product is the odd one: it is not symmetric in its factors.

The Cartesian product

Files a to d are a path of four; ranks 1 to 3 are a path of three. Their Cartesian product has a square for every file and rank, and it moves like a rook taking one step: change the file or the rank by one step, not both. Press Step to draw its moves.

The product is a lazy view. Each step draws one more of its edges, in the order the view lists them.

The ideaA vertex for every pair, an edge by the rule

Write a square as a pair: its file and its rank. In the Cartesian product two squares are joined when they share a file and their ranks are adjacent, or share a rank and their files are adjacent. That is a rook moving one step, and the product of two paths is a grid. In the tensor product both coordinates must change, each to a neighbour: a bishop’s single step. The strong product allows either: a king. The lexicographic product lets a move change the file to a neighbouring file and land on any rank at all, or keep the file and move the rank by one.

Grafluent builds each as a lazy view over the two factors: cartesianProductView(files, ranks) and its three siblings. A view stores nothing but its factors. Its vertices are Pairs, its edges come from the factors’ edges, and its rows are computed when read, so a product with a million vertices costs no more memory than its factors.

Products compose with everything else. A breadth-first search on the Cartesian product of two paths measures rook distance; on the strong product it measures king distance, which is the larger of the two coordinate differences.

Where it breaksCounting grows fast

A product with n₁ n₂ vertices is easy to build but no easier to search. Every algorithm that visits all of it still pays for all of it, and three factors multiply again. Directed factors give directed products, with the same rules read along the arcs. Self-loops in a factor make the products join a square to itself; the views follow their factors and keep them.

import AdjacencyListModule
import GraphProducts
import GraphProtocols

let files = UndirectedAdjacencyList<String> {
    UndirectedEdge("a", "b"); UndirectedEdge("b", "c"); UndirectedEdge("c", "d")
}

let ranks = UndirectedAdjacencyList<String> {
    UndirectedEdge("1", "2"); UndirectedEdge("2", "3")
}

let rook = cartesianProductView(files, ranks)
let squares = rook.vertexCount  // 12
let steps = rook.edgeCount  // 17
let from = rook.neighbors(of: Pair("b", "2")).map { $0.first + $0.second }  // ["a2", "c2", "b1", "b3"]
