Resilience

A data centre has twelve racks and twenty patches. How many can fail at once before some rack is cut off, and which racks hold together best?

What it’s forHow much failure a network takes

A network’s vertex connectivity is the fewest vertices whose failure splits it; its edge connectivity is the fewest links. A minimum vertex cut names such a set. The k-components refine this inside the network: the largest groups that stay connected through any k − 1 failures among their own members, for every k.

Reach for them to rate a network’s robustness, to find where to add a link so one failure no longer isolates a site, or to find the tightly knit cores of a social network, which is what Moody and White built k-components for.

Bridges and articulation points answer the k = 1 question fast. Connectivity and k-components answer it for every k, at a price that grows with the network.

The ideaCount the disjoint paths

By Menger’s theorem, the fewest vertices whose failure separates two others equals the most paths between them that share no vertex. So connectivity is a maximum flow question: split each vertex into an in-half and an out-half joined by a link of capacity one, and the largest flow between two vertices is the number of vertex-disjoint paths. vertexConnectivity() takes the least over the pairs it must check, and minimumVertexCut() reads the cut off the flow. edgeConnectivity() does the same without splitting vertices.

kComponents() builds the hierarchy by cohesive blocking: find the connectivity k of a piece, cut it at a minimum cut, and continue in each part until it falls apart. Every group at k lies inside a group at k − 1, and the 2-components are the blocks with three or more vertices. In the centre, each ring of four is a 3-component, because a ring of four cross-linked racks is K₄ and survives any two failures. The two panels join the rings into a 2-component. The lab and the desk, in a loop through two racks of one ring, belong to the 2-component but to no 3-component.

Where it breaksA flow per pair adds up

Each connectivity question is a maximum flow, and k-components ask many of them, so on a network of thousands of vertices they take a while and on millions they are impractical. For a single point of failure use bridges and articulation points instead, which cost one pass. The numbers also say nothing about likelihood: a cut of two racks that share a power supply is worse than its size suggests.

import AdjacencyListModule
import Connectivity
import Flows
import GraphProtocols

/// Two rings of four racks, each ring fully cross-linked, joined by two patch panels, with a lab
/// and a desk looped through one ring.
let patches = [
    UndirectedEdge("R1", "R2"), UndirectedEdge("R1", "R3"), UndirectedEdge("R1", "R4"), UndirectedEdge("R2", "R3"),
    UndirectedEdge("R2", "R4"), UndirectedEdge("R3", "R4"),
    UndirectedEdge("S1", "S2"), UndirectedEdge("S1", "S3"), UndirectedEdge("S1", "S4"), UndirectedEdge("S2", "S3"),
    UndirectedEdge("S2", "S4"), UndirectedEdge("S3", "S4"),
    UndirectedEdge("R4", "P1"), UndirectedEdge("R3", "P2"), UndirectedEdge("P1", "S1"), UndirectedEdge("P2", "S2"),
    UndirectedEdge("P1", "P2"),
    UndirectedEdge("S4", "Lab"), UndirectedEdge("Lab", "Desk"), UndirectedEdge("Desk", "S3"),
]

let machineRoom = UndirectedAdjacencyList<String> {
    for patch in patches { patch }
}

let racks = machineRoom.vertexConnectivity()  // 2
let links = machineRoom.edgeConnectivity()  // 2
let weakest = machineRoom.minimumVertexCut()  // ["S2", "P1"]

let cohesion = machineRoom.kComponents()
let levels = (1 ... cohesion.maximumK).map { k in cohesion[k].map(Array.init) }  // [[["R1", "R2", "R3", "R4", "S1", "S2", "S3", "S4", "P1", "P2", "Lab", "Desk"]], [["R1", "R2", "R3", "R4", "S1", "S2", "S3", "S4", "P1", "P2", "Lab", "Desk"]], [["R1", "R2", "R3", "R4"], ["S1", "S2", "S3", "S4"]]]
