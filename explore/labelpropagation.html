Label propagation

If everyone repeats whatever most of their friends say, which groups end up saying the same thing?

Example: sixteen children on a playground and who plays with whom. Each starts with a rumour of their own, then keeps switching to the one most of their friends tell. When nobody wants to switch, the children who tell the same rumour are a community.

The ideaNo score, just a local rule

Louvain and Girvan–Newman score whole partitions and look for a good one. Label propagation never scores anything. Each vertex looks only at its neighbours and takes the label most of them have, and inside a tightly knit group that one label quickly wins. Between groups there are too few edges for a label to cross. A sweep costs one look at every edge, and a handful of sweeps is usually enough, so it scales to networks of millions of vertices where scoring would be slow.

The order matters. Going round one child at a time, as asynchronousLabelPropagationCommunities() does, means later children hear the changes earlier ones made in the same sweep. Updating everyone at once instead can make two friends swap labels with each other for ever, so labelPropagationCommunities() updates in rounds of children who aren’t friends (the colour classes of a colouring), which always settles.

Where it breaksTies, and one rumour for everyone

Ties are everywhere at the start, when every rumour has exactly one teller, so the tie rule and the order decide a lot. Grafluent’s in-order form breaks ties by taking the last label in the list, which makes it repeatable but arbitrary; the random form breaks them by chance, so two seeds can give two different answers, as Draw again shows. Neither is wrong: the method has no notion of a best answer.

On a network without clear groups, or with groups joined by many links, one rumour can flood everything and leave a single community. Label propagation is best as a fast first look at a large network, with a method that measures quality, such as modularity, to check what it found.
