Euler tours

Say itOY-ler/ˈɔɪlər/andHEER-holt-ser/ˈhiːɐ̯hɔlt͡sɐ/

Can you walk across every bridge in town exactly once?

What it’s forEvery edge, exactly once

An Euler tour is a walk that uses every edge of a graph exactly once. Counting the edges at each place tells you at once whether a tour exists. When one does, Hierholzer’s algorithm finds it in time in proportion to the edges.

Reach for it to plan a route that must cover every street, such as for a snowplough or a street sweeper. It also draws a figure without lifting the pen, and it rebuilds a DNA sequence from overlapping fragments.

Example: Königsberg in 1736, with four pieces of land joined by seven bridges. Each piece of land is a vertex and each bridge an edge. Two pairs of banks are joined by two bridges each. Those are separate edges, so the graph is a multigraph. Then try the same city with an eighth bridge, and a small town where a tour exists.

import GraphProtocols
import Multigraphs
import Tours
import Walks

let bridges = [
    UndirectedEdge("North bank", "Kneiphof"),
    UndirectedEdge("North bank", "Kneiphof"),
    UndirectedEdge("South bank", "Kneiphof"),
    UndirectedEdge("South bank", "Kneiphof"),
    UndirectedEdge("Kneiphof", "Lomse"),
    UndirectedEdge("North bank", "Lomse"),
    UndirectedEdge("South bank", "Lomse"),
]

let city = Multigraph<String> {
    for bridge in bridges { bridge }
}!

let odd = city.vertices.filter { city.degree(of: $0) % 2 == 1 }  // ["North bank", "Kneiphof", "South bank", "Lomse"]
let circuit = city.eulerianCircuit()  // nil
let trail = city.eulerianTrail()  // nil

The ideaCount the bridges at each place

The people of Königsberg tried for years to cross all seven bridges in one walk, never crossing one twice. In 1736 Euler showed that they never would, without trying a single route. Each time a walk arrives at a piece of land over one bridge, it leaves over another. So the bridges at a place are used in pairs. Only the places where the walk starts and ends may have a bridge left over.

So a walk over every bridge exactly once needs at most two places with an odd count. With none, the walk can end where it began. That is an Eulerian circuit. With exactly two, it must start at one and end at the other. That is an Eulerian trail. Königsberg has four odd places, so neither exists. Add an eighth bridge between the two banks, and only Kneiphof and Lomse stay odd. Now a walk exists, from one to the other.

Counting proves a tour impossible. Finding one when the counts allow it is Hierholzer’s part, published in 1873. Walk from the start, always over an unused bridge. Every other place has its bridges in pairs, so you can only get stuck back at the start. If bridges are left over, some place on the walk still has one. Walk a second loop from there, and stitch it into the first at that place.

Grafluent runs the stack form of this. Whenever it gets stuck, it backs up, and each bridge it backs over joins the tour from its end. The detours are stitched in without extra work. Each bridge is handled at most twice, so the time is in proportion to the number of bridges.

An Euler tour is the cheapest to find, but a graph often has none. Then a delivery round that must cover every street has to walk some of them twice. On one-way streets the rule changes. Every place needs as many streets in as out, which isEulerian checks on a directed graph.
